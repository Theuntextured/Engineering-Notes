## Gradient

For $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R}$

$$
\begin{align}
\nabla f(\vec{x})=\left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}},\dots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}} \right) &  & \vec{x}\in \mathbb{R}^n &  & \implies \nabla f:\mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R}^n
\end{align}
$$

> [!WARNING]+
> Differentiability of a function $f:\mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}$ at point $x$ implies continuity. However, for $g:\mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R},n>1$, partial differentiability in one direction does **NOT** imply continuity.

### Differentiability

A function $f$ is differentiable at $P_{0}$ if:

$$
f(P)=f(P_{0})+\nabla f(P_{0})\cdot(P-P_{0})+\mathcal{||P-P_{0}||}
$$
> [!TIP]+ Notice
> The above formula is simply **Taylor Expansion**!

If $f\in C^1(D)$, then $f$ is differentiable in $D$.

# Directional Derivatives

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial \vec{v}}(P):=\lim_{ h \to 0 } \frac{{f(P+h\vec{v})-f(P)}}{h}
$$

$$
\frac{{\partial f}}{\partial \vec{v}}(P_{0})=\nabla f(P_{0})\cdot \vec{v}
$$

## Tangent Plane of a Function

$\Pi$ is the tangent plane of $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R}$ at point $P_{0}$:

$$
\Pi:z=f(P_{0})+\nabla f(P_{0})\cdot(P-P_{0})
$$

Normal vector of plane:

$$
N:=(\nabla f(P_{0}),-1)
$$

## Hessian Matrix & Taylor Expansion

$$
Hf:=\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\partial y} \\
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\partial x} & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

> [!NOTE]+
> Let $f:D\to \mathbb{R}$ for $D\subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$
> 
> $$
> f\in C^2\implies Hf=(Hf)^T
> $$
> 
> i.e. It is symmetric if $f$'s second order derivatives are all continuous.

> [!GRAPH]+ 2nd order Taylor Polynomial  around point $P_{0}$
> $$
> T_{f,2}(P)= f({P_{0}})+\nabla f(P_{0})\cdot(P-P_{0})+\frac{1}{2}(P-P_{0})\cdot Hf(P-P_{0})\cdot (P-P_{0})^T
> $$

# Critical Points
*And min/max*

> [!SUMMARY]+ Fermat's Theorem
> 
> We say that $P_{0}$ is a critical point for $f$ if $\nabla f(P_{0})=\vec{0}$. The theorem therefore states that:
> 
> For a function $f\in C^1$, if $P_{0}$ is a local min/max of $f$ $\implies$ $P_{0}$ is a **critical point** for $f$.
> 
> > [!NOTE] The opposite does not always hold. 
> > In the same way as $x=0$ is not a min or max point for $f(x)=x^3$. In multiple variables, these are called **saddle points**.
> 
> > [!WARNING]
> > It is important to check that $f\in C^1$, since the theorem does not hold otherwise. For example in $f(P)=\sqrt{ x^2+y^2 }$ (double cone trace) around $P_{0}=(0,0)$. This is because partial derivatives are not defined at $(0,0)$, so it cannot be classified as a critical point.

### Classification of Critical Points

Let $D\subseteq \mathbb{R}^2,f\in C^2(D)$ and $P_{0}\in D$ be a critical point for $f$. Then,

- If $\det Hf(P_{0})>0\implies$ $P_{0}$ is a local min/max
	- $\frac{{\partial^2f}}{\partial x^2}(P_{0})>0\implies$ $P_{0}$ is a local min
	- $\frac{{\partial^2f}}{\partial x^2}(P_{0})<0\implies$ $P_{0}$ is a local max
- If $\det Hf(P_{0})<0\implies$ $P_{0}$ is a **saddle point**
- If $\det Hf(P_{0})=0\implies$ NO INFO GIVEN FROM THIS METHOD!

### 2D classification Using Hessian Matrix

Let $\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}$ be the eigenvalues of $Hf(P_{0})$ with respective eigenvectors $\vec{v}_{1},\vec{v}_{2}$

Then, we can classify critical points:
1. If $\lambda_{1}\cdot \lambda_{2}>0\implies P_{0}\text{ is a local min or max.}$
	1. If $\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0\implies P_{0}\text{ is a local minimum point for f}$
	2. If $\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}<0\implies P_{0}\text{ is a local maximum point for f}$
2. If $\lambda_{1}\cdot \lambda_{2}<0\implies P_{0}\text{ is a saddle point}$
3. $\lambda_{1}\cdot \lambda_{2}=0$ gives no useful information

> [!CHECK]+ $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R}$
> This extends to $n$ dimensions, but if any eigenvalue is $0$, the method yields no useful information. 
> A saddle point is identified by two eigenvaleus of opposite signs being present.
