## Measurement Fundamentals
* **Measurement:** A process to quantify the value of a quantity of interest. The output is a number and a unit, *always* associated with its measurement uncertainty.
* **Operationalism:** The very meaning of a quantity is defined by the method used to measure it (Bridgman). 
    * Different methods can measure the same quantity, which is acceptable because there is no single "true value". Measurement uncertainty determines if the different methods are compatible.

## The SI Base Units
The International System of Units (SI) defines 7 base units based on universal physical constants:
1. Time: second ($s$) - Caesium 133 frequency ($\Delta \nu_{Cs}$)
2. Length: metre ($m$) - Speed of light ($c$)
3. Mass: kilogram ($kg$) - Planck's constant ($h$)
4. Luminous intensity: candela ($cd$) - Luminous efficacy ($K_{cd}$)
5. Amount of substance: mole ($mol$) - Avogadro constant ($N_A$)
6. Thermodynamic temperature: kelvin ($K$) - Boltzmann constant ($k$)
7. Electric current: ampère ($A$) - Elementary charge ($e$)

> [!INFO] Metre Realization
> The metre is no longer a strictly independent base unit because it is defined using the second (time is the quantity measured with the lowest uncertainty). A laser source with a calibrated frequency $f_o$ acts as a ruler, where wavelength $\lambda_o = c / f_o$. Length is measured by counting wavelengths using interferometry.

## Influence Factors in Length Measurement
The physical length measurement process is affected by several external factors:

### 1. Temperature Effects
* **Thermal Expansion:** A homogeneous change in dimension. Can be mathematically corrected if the thermal expansion coefficient ($\alpha$) and temperature ($T$) are known: $L_{20} - L_T = \alpha L_T (20 - T)$.
    * *Note:* If the measuring instrument also expands (coefficient $\beta$), the model becomes $L_{20} - L_T = (\alpha - \beta) L_T (20 - T)$.
* **Thermal Distortion:** Non-homogeneous heating (e.g., heat from an operator's hand on a micrometer frame) causes a change in *shape*. This ruins the parallelism of measuring surfaces (anvil and spindle) and causes severe errors that cannot be easily corrected.

### 2. Force Effects
* Based on Hooke's law of elasticity.
* **Compression (Dimension Variation):** A gauge block standing vertically shrinks due to its own weight. ISO requires blocks $\le 100$ mm to be calibrated vertically (compression is negligible).
* **Bending (Shape Variation):** Blocks $> 100$ mm are calibrated horizontally. They must be placed on specific supports to minimize bending errors:
    * **Airy Points ($a = 0.2887 L$):** End faces remain parallel (rotations at the ends are null).
    * **Bessel Points ($a = 0.2779 L$):** Minimizes total deflection (used when measuring the whole length, not just the end faces).
* **Contact Forces (Micrometer):** The ratchet stop limits static torque, but rapid operation introduces inertial forces that the ratchet cannot control, leading to systematic errors between operators. Small contact surfaces (e.g., sphere on plane) also require Hertzian contact formulas to evaluate local deformation.

### 3. Interaction Errors
* **Alignment Error (Cosine Error):** The measurement axis is misaligned with the measurand by angle $\theta$. $L_m = L \cos \theta \approx L\left( 1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2} \right)$.
* **Position Error (Sine Error):** Non-parallel measuring jaws. The error is $\Delta L = d \sin \alpha \approx d \cdot \alpha$, where $d$ is the jaw length. 
    * **Abbe's Principle:** The measuring scale should be superimposed on (or directly in line with) the measurand to avoid this. Calipers and CMMs violate Abbe's principle, while micrometers follow it.
* **Probe Form Errors:** CMMs measure the center of their spherical probe, not the contact point. If the probe radius ($r$) has a qualification error ($e_t$), it causes:
    * **Inside measurements:** Error is $+2e_t$.
    * **Outside measurements:** Error is $-2e_t$.
    * Probing a non-orthogonal surface is highly critical because the machine must guess the contact direction based on the piece's geometry.

## Typical Measurement Errors
* **Zero Error ($A$):** A constant absolute error, independent of the measured length (e.g., placing the ruler starting at the 5mm mark).
* **Sensitivity Error ($B \cdot L$):** A relative error proportional to the measured length (e.g., thermal elongation of the ruler itself).
* Total deviation is often modelled as the binomial: $A + B \cdot L$.
