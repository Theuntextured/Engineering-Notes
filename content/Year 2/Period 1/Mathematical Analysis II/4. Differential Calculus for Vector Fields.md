# Divergence
Let $F=(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})\in C^1(D;\mathbb{R}^n)$

Divergence of $F$ is defined as:

$$
\text{div }F=\sum \frac{{\partial F_{k}}}{\partial x_{k}}=\nabla \cdot F
$$

> [!TLDR]+ 2D simplification
> $$
> F=(F_1, F_{2})\implies \text{div }F=\frac{{\partial F_{1}}}{\partial x}+\frac{{\partial F_{2}}}{\partial y}
> $$

> [!GRAPH]+ Visualization
> ![[Pasted image 20260731170553.png]]
> 
> Divergence can be visualized as the net flow of a vector field into a point. 
> - $\text{div }F>0$: $F$ is locally outflowing from $P_{0}$
>   $\text{div F}<0$: $F$ is locally inflowing into $P_{0}$
> - $\text{div }F=0$: There is no net flow. An example of this is for any control volume measuring magnetic field.

# Curl
### 2D case
Let $F\in C^1(\mathbb{R}^2;\mathbb{R}^2)$. We set:

$$
\text{curl } F:=\frac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial x}-\frac{{\partial F_{1}}}{\partial y}
$$

### 3D case
Let $F\in C^1(\mathbb{R}^3;\mathbb{R}^3)$. We set:

$$
\begin{align}
\text{curl }F := & \begin{vmatrix}
\vec{i} & \vec{j} & \vec{k} \\
\frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\
F_{1} & F_{2} & F_{3}
\end{vmatrix} \\
= & \left( \frac{\partial F_{3}}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial z}   ,\frac{\partial F_{1}}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial F_{3}}{\partial x},\frac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial F_{1}}{\partial y}    \right) \\
= & \nabla \times F
\end{align}
$$

> [!EXAMPLE]-
> ![[Pasted image 20260731181146.png]]

- $\text{curl }F< 0$: $F$ is locally rotating around the point clockwise
- $\text{curl }>0$: $F$ is locally rotating around the point counterclockwise
- $\text{curl }F=0$: No rotation

> [!IMPORTANT]+ Rules
> Let $f\in C^2(\mathbb{R}^3),F\in C^2(\mathbb{R}^3;\mathbb{R}^3)$
> 
> $$
> \begin{align}
> \text{curl }(\nabla f)=\vec{0} &  & \text{div }(\text{curl }F)=0  &  & \text{curl }(fF)=\nabla f\times F+f\text{curl }F
> \end{align}
> $$

# Jacobian Matrix
Given:

$$
f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots, x_{n})=(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{m})
$$

We define the Jacobian matrix as:

$$
\begin{align}
Df & =\begin{pmatrix}
\nabla f_{1} \\
\nabla f_{2} \\
\vdots \\
\nabla f_{m}
\end{pmatrix} \\
 & =\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}} & \frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{2}}   & \dots & \frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{n}}  \\
\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{1}} & \frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}   & \dots & \frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{n}}  \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{1}} & \frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{2}}   & \dots  & \frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{n}} 
\end{pmatrix}\in \mathbb{R}^{m,n}
\end{align}
$$

The Jacobian can be used to give a Taylor approximation to a function (around the origin in the example):

$$
F(x,y)\approx F(0,0)+DF(0,0)\cdot \begin{pmatrix}
x \\
y
\end{pmatrix}+o(||(x,y)||)
$$
