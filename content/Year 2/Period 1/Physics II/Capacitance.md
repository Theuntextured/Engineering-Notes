Total charge of a capacitor is ALWAYS zero.

![[Pasted image 20260205220702.png]]

In an ideal capacitor, outside, we have$E=0$, and between the plates:

$$
\begin{align}
&E=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon }=\frac{Q}{\varepsilon A} \\
\implies &\Delta V=\frac{Qd}{\varepsilon A} \\
&C=\frac{\varepsilon A}{d} \\
&U=\frac{1}{2} \frac{Q^2}{C}=\frac{1}{2}C\Delta V^2=\frac{1}{2}Q\Delta V
\end{align}
$$
$$
U=\int_{V} \frac{1}{2} \varepsilon E^2dV
$$
If we charge a capacitor and then vary $C$, we have two cases:

**Constant $\Delta V$:**

$$
\Delta U=\frac{1}{2}\Delta C\Delta V^2
$$
**Constant $q$:**

$$
\Delta U=\frac{1}{2} \Delta \left( \frac{1}{C} \right)Q^2
$$
If we place capacitors in **parallel**, we get:

$$
C_{eq}=\Sigma C_{i}
$$
And in **series**:
$$
\frac{1}{C_{eq}}=\Sigma {\frac{1}{C_{i}}}
$$
