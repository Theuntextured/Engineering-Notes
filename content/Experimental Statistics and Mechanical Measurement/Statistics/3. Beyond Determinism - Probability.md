
## Determinism vs Randomness
* Determinism relies on exact relationships, but exact results cannot be obtained from doubtful data.
* Traditional engineering uses “safety coefficients” to cover ignorance/irregularities, but this only works for standardized schemes.
* Probability and statistics are required to manage non-deterministic variables (random variables) in modern practice.

## Basic Definitions
* **Random Phenomenon:** An empirical phenomenon where the result cannot be determined a priori.
* **Sample Space ($S$):** The set of ALL possible outcomes. 
* **Cardinality ($\#S$):** The numerosity (total count) of the sample space.
* **Event ($E$):** A subset of the sample space ($E \subset S$).
* **Event Space ($A$):** The set of all possible events.

## Concepts of Probability
| Type | Nature | Definition / Application |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Classical** | Objective, A priori | Requires enumeration of all possible, equally likely events. |
| **Frequentist** | Objective, A posteriori | Does NOT require enumerating all events; relies on real experimental trials. |
| **Bayesian** | Subjective | Trustworthiness degree of a belief; used when enumeration and infinite trials are impossible. |

### 1. Classical Probability
* Ratio of favourable events to total possible events.
* Assumes all results are equally possible and incompatible.

$$
\mathbb{P}[E] = \frac{\#E}{\#S}
$$

* Note: $\mathbb{P}[E]$ is always between $0$ and $1$ ($0 \le \mathbb{P}[E] \le 1$).

### 2. Frequentist Probability
* Evaluated by repeating trials $N$ times and counting the absolute frequency of the event ($n_E$).
* Relative frequency $f_E = n_E / N$.
* Requires a large sample size; experimental ratios change randomly per experiment.

$$
\mathbb{P}[E] = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{n_E}{N}
$$

### 3. Bayesian Probability
* Leverages Bayes' theorem to update the probability of a hypothesis iteratively.
* Combines **Prior Assumption** + **Likelihood of Occurrence (Data)** $\rightarrow$ **Posterior Distribution**.

## Combinatorics 
Used to determine cardinalities for classical probability calculations.

> [!INFO] Permutations vs. Combinations
> **Permutations:** Order MATTERS.
> **Combinations:** Order DOES NOT MATTER.

**Permutations Formula:**

$$
\mathcal{P}_n = n!
$$

**Combinations Formula:**

$$
\mathcal{C}(n,k) = \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
$$

## Compound Probability (Venn Diagrams)
* **Kolmogorov Axioms:**
    1. $0 \le \mathbb{P}[E_i] \le 1$
    2. $\mathbb{P}[S] = 1$
    3. $\mathbb{P}[\bar{E}] = 1 - \mathbb{P}[E]$

### Union of Events
* **Incompatible (Disjoint) Events:** No elements in common ($EF = \emptyset$).

$$
\mathbb{P}(E \cup F) = \mathbb{P}(E) + \mathbb{P}(F)
$$

* **Compatible Events:** Share common elements (must subtract the intersection to avoid double-counting).

$$
\mathbb{P}(E \cup F) = \mathbb{P}(E) + \mathbb{P}(F) - \mathbb{P}(EF)
$$

* **Three Compatible Events:** Add single probabilities, subtract double intersections, add back the triple intersection.

$$
\mathbb{P}(E \cup F \cup G) = \mathbb{P}(E) + \mathbb{P}(F) + \mathbb{P}(G) - \mathbb{P}(EF) - \mathbb{P}(FG) - \mathbb{P}(EG) + \mathbb{P}(EFG)
$$

## Tree Diagrams
* Used for sequential/complex probabilities where combinatorics is too difficult.
* Branches must be broken down into mutually exclusive (incompatible) events.
* **Probability of a single path:** Multiply probabilities along the branches.
* **Total probability:** Sum the probabilities of all valid paths.

## Conditional Probability
* Probability of event $E$ occurring *given that* event $F$ has already occurred.

$$
\mathbb{P}(E|F) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(EF)}{\mathbb{P}(F)}
$$

* Which rearranges to find the joint probability:

$$
\mathbb{P}(EF) = \mathbb{P}(F) \cdot \mathbb{P}(E|F)
$$

### Independent Events
* The occurrence of $F$ does NOT affect the probability of $E$ (and vice versa).
* $\mathbb{P}(E|F) = \mathbb{P}(E)$.

$$
\mathbb{P}(EF) = \mathbb{P}(E)\mathbb{P}(F)
$$

---

## Total Probability Theorem
* Let $B_i$ be a complete partition of $S$ (mutually exclusive and exhaustive sets). 
* Let $A$ be an event that intersects with $B_i$.

$$
\mathbb{P}(A) = \sum_{i} \mathbb{P}(B_i) \cdot \mathbb{P}(A|B_i)
$$

---

## Bayes' Formula
* Reverses conditional probability to find the probability of a specific cause ($B_i$) given that the result ($A$) has occurred.

$$
\mathbb{P}(B_i|A) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(B_i) \cdot \mathbb{P}(A|B_i)}{\sum_{k} \mathbb{P}(B_k) \cdot \mathbb{P}(A|B_k)}
$$
