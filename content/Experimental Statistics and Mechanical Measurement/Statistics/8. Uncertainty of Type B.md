## Limitations of Pure Statistics (Type A)
* Purely statistical methods require a remarkably large number of experimental data points to estimate population parameters accurately.
* The experimental sample must be perfectly representative of all factors affecting the population, which is expensive and requires expert oversight to ensure no factors are missed.

## GUM Framework: Type A vs. Type B
The "Guide to the expression of uncertainty in measurement" (GUM) establishes two evaluation methods to find the **standard uncertainty** (**u**):

| Type       | Evaluation Method | Basis                                                                   |
| :--------- | :---------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Type A** | Statistical       | Repeated experimental observations.                                     |
| **Type B** | Non-Statistical   | Prior knowledge, technical handbooks, accuracy classes, and experience. |

> [!WARNING] Type A is NOT inherently better
> GUM explicitly states that Type A evaluations are not necessarily superior to Type B. In practical situations where experimental observations are limited, Type B evaluations may actually be more reliable than Type A.

## Common Type B Uncertainty Sources
* **Handbook Numerical Data:** If a number is given without tolerance, the variability affects its least significant digit. For technical instruments, it is reasonable to assume a variability of **±2** units of that least significant digit.
* **Instrument Bias / Specs:** When only an accuracy class or technical standard (e.g., ISO conformity) is known, it provides a variability interval (e.g., maximum error of **±4 µm**) rather than an actual measurement spread. 

## Type B Distributions & Equivalent Variances
Type B uncertainties are usually given as a variability range **±a**. You must convert this range into an "equivalent variance" (**u²**) using one of three specific distributions:

### 1. Uniform (Rectangular) Distribution
* **Use Case:** Adopted when absolutely nothing is known about the behavior of the uncertainty factor within its variability range **±a**. 
* **Common Example:** Reading resolution of analogue or digital scales (any value between the resolution ticks is equally probable).

$$
u^2 = \frac{a^2}{3}
$$

### 2. Arcsine (U-Shaped) Distribution
* **Use Case:** Adopted when the influencing factor varies sinusoidally over time.
* **Common Example:** Daily natural temperature or humidity oscillations in an unconditioned environment over 24 hours. The temperature spends more time at the extremes than in the middle, resulting in a U-shaped probability density.

$$
u^2 = \frac{a^2}{2}
$$

### 3. Triangular Distribution
* **Use Case:** Adopted when it is reasonable to assume values closer to the center are more probable, but the exact distribution shape remains unknown.
* **Note:** Generally not recommended for inexpert users.

$$
u^2 = \frac{a^2}{6}
$$

## Planning with Type B Uncertainty
* Type B evaluation is highly beneficial during the initial planning stage of an experiment, before any experimental data is available.
* It allows engineers to associate an equivalent variance to known technical specifications (e.g., resolution, accuracy class, handbook thermal expansion coefficients).
* Thanks to the Central Limit Theorem, these Type B variances can be seamlessly combined with Type A variances later for a complete, statistical management of total uncertainty.
