## Moving Beyond Normality Tests
* Normality tests (like $\chi^2$ and NPP) warn that systematic factors exist, but they do not *identify* what those factors are.
* To find the root cause, data must be grouped homogeneously by the suspected factor (e.g., separating data by operator or machine).
* **Hypothesis Testing** and **ANOVA** are then used to determine if the differences between these groups are systematic or merely random.

## Hypothesis Testing
* **The Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** Always assumes there is NO significant/systematic difference (i.e., any variation is just random). 
* If the test *rejects* $H_0$, it means a systematic effect is present.

### 4 Steps of Hypothesis Testing
1. Group data analytically to isolate the suspected factor.
2. Choose the theoretical statistical distribution.
3. Set the acceptable risk of error ($\alpha$) and confidence level ($P = 1 - \alpha$).
4. Determine the boundaries of the non-rejection zone (Confidence Interval).

### Tests for the Mean
* **z-test (Overall Homogeneity):** Tests if individual sample averages belong to the global distribution.
    * Standard deviation of the averages ($q$ = number of results per group):

$$
s_m = \frac{s}{\sqrt{q}}
$$

* *Logical Bug of the z-test:* It uses the standard deviation $s$ of the *entire* dataset to build the confidence interval. If a severe systematic effect exists, $s$ is artificially inflated, which might mask the systematic error.
* **t-test (Pairwise Comparison):** Overcomes the z-test's logical bug. 
    * It computes the confidence interval for *each* sample independently using Student's t-distribution. 
    * If the confidence intervals of two samples do not overlap, a systematic difference is confirmed.

### Test for the Variance
* Checks if the dispersion/spread of data is homogeneous across groups.
* Uses Pearson's $\chi^2$ distribution to evaluate the sample variance $s_i^2$:

$$
v_i = (q-1)\frac{s_i^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{(q-1)}
$$

## Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)
* Evaluates the ratio between two variance calculations:
    * **Numerator:** Variance *between* groups (affected by the systematic factor).
    * **Denominator:** Variance *within* groups (affected ONLY by random errors).
* **Pros:** Can handle multiple factors simultaneously; uses an unpolluted reference (the denominator) for comparison.
* **Cons:** Tells you *which factor* is causing systematic error, but not *which specific element* of the factor (e.g., it tells you "Operators" are the problem, but not which specific operator is messing up).

### Single-Factor ANOVA
* Decomposes the Total Sum of Squares ($SS_{TC}$) into: Sum of Squares Between ($SS_B$) and Sum of Squares Within ($SS_W$).

$$
SS_{TC} = SS_W + SS_B
$$

* **Variance Between Groups ($s_B^2$):** ($k$ = number of groups, $n$ = items per group)

$$
s_B^2 = \frac{SS_B}{k-1} = \frac{n \sum_{j=1}^{k} (\overline{x}_{\bullet j} - \overline{x}_{\bullet\bullet})^2}{k-1}
$$

* **Variance Within Groups ($s_W^2$):**

$$
s_W^2 = \frac{SS_W}{k(n-1)} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{ij} - \overline{x}_{\bullet j})^2}{k(n-1)}
$$

* **Variance Ratio ($F_{exp}$):**

$$
F_{exp} = \frac{s_B^2}{s_W^2}
$$

* **Decision Rule:** Compare $F_{exp}$ to the Fisher upper limit ($F_U$). If $F_{exp} > F_U$, reject the null hypothesis (the factor is systematically affecting results).

### Two-Factor ANOVA
* Decomposes the Total Sum of Squares into three parts: Columns ($SS_{Bc}$), Rows ($SS_{Br}$), and Within/Random ($SS_W$).

$$
SS_{TC} = SS_{Bc} + SS_{Br} + SS_W
$$

* You compute separate variance ratios for Factor 1 ($s_{Bc}^2 / s_W^2$) and Factor 2 ($s_{Br}^2 / s_W^2$) and compare them to their respective Fisher limits.

> [!WARNING] The Blind Spot of ANOVA
> ANOVA evaluates discrete groups/intervals. If there is a continuous, long-term systematic effect (like tool wear or a slow thermal drift), ANOVA might miss it because it segments the drift into chunks. Always cross-reference with linear regression or temporal plots.
