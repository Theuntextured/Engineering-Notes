## The Concept of Expanded Uncertainty
* **Standard Uncertainty ($u_c$):** Corresponds to standard deviation, which only covers a portion of the possible values (~68% for a normal distribution).
* **Expanded Uncertainty ($U$):** Provides a wider, "reasonable" coverage interval based on a statistical "confidence level" ($P$).
* **Formula:** 

$$
U = k \cdot u_c(y)
$$

* $k$ is the **coverage factor**.
* In metrology, it is conventionally assumed $k=2$, which corresponds to approximately a 95% confidence level.
* *Note:* If the term "uncertainty" is used alone in technical literature (per GUM), it implicitly means "expanded uncertainty".

## The Calculation Procedure
1. **Set the Risk of Error ($\alpha$):** $\alpha = 1 - P$. Conventionally $P = 95\%$, so $\alpha = 5\%$ (bilateral symmetrical risk).
2. **Determine Coverage Factor ($k$):** Use the number of degrees of freedom ($v_y$) to find $k$ using either $z_{max}$ (Normal distribution) or $t_{max}$ (Student's t-distribution). 
    * *Best Practice:* Use Student's t-distribution to avoid underestimating the uncertainty.
3. **Format the Result:** The final measurement must be reported as:

$$
y = \bar{y} \pm U(y)
$$

## Back-Calculating Standard Uncertainty
Often, you receive equipment specs with an expanded uncertainty ($U$) and must extract the standard uncertainty ($s$) for your budget.

| Provided Info              | Procedure / Assumption                                                                        |
| :------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **$P$ and $v$ known**      | Calculate using the precise Student t value.                                                  |
| **$v$ known, $P$ unknown** | Assume the conventional $P = 95\%$.                                                           |
| **$P$ known, $v$ unknown** | Assume Normal distribution (use $z$). Conventionally assign $v_d = 5$ for degrees of freedom. |
| **Nothing known**          | Assume $2\sigma$ level ($P = 95\%$, $k = 2$) and conventionally assign $v_d = 5$.             |

## Degrees of Freedom ($v$)
Acts as a measure of reliability for the uncertainty contributions.

* **Type A (Experimental):** $v = \text{number of data points} - \text{number of constraints}$.
    * Mean of $n$ data $\rightarrow v = n - 1$.
    * Regression with $n_p$ parameters $\rightarrow v = n - n_p$.
* **Type B (Non-Statistical):** Theoretically $\infty$, but practically assigned based on reliability:
    * $v = 100 \rightarrow$ Extremely consolidated info.
    * $v = 30 \rightarrow$ Consolidated info.
    * $v = 15 \rightarrow$ Info to be checked.

### Welch-Satterthwaite Formula
Used to calculate the combined degrees of freedom ($v_y$) of the dependent variable $Y$ from multiple independent contributions ($v_j$):

$$
v_y = \frac{u^4(y)}{\sum \frac{u_j^4(y)}{v_j}}
$$

## PUMA Method (ISO 14253-2:2011)
*Procedure for Uncertainty Management* to plan/validate a measurement process iteratively.

* **Step 1:** Define the objective (acceptable $U$), define the measurement procedure, and set up the uncertainty budget.
* **Step 2:** Start with a pessimistic, rough overestimation of Type A and B contributions.
    * $\rightarrow$ If $U$ is acceptable: **Done.** (Saves time/money).
    * $\rightarrow$ If $U$ is NOT acceptable: Refine calculation with more exact/realistic data.
    * $\rightarrow$ If still NOT acceptable: Modify the measurement procedure or rethink the core objectives.

## Design of Measurement Procedure
* Different physical models for finding the same measurand (e.g., center distance between two holes) yield different combined uncertainties and require different amounts of work.
* **Optimization:** The model yielding the absolute lowest uncertainty is not always the best choice; you must balance uncertainty requirements against operational costs (e.g., number of measurements required).
