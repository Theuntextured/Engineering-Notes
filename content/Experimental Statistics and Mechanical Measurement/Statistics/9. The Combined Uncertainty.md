## Metrological vs. Mathematical Models
* **Mathematical Model:** The basic physical equation representing the relationship of variables (e.g., $L_{20} = L_T[1 + \alpha(20 - T)]$).
* **Metrological Model:** Expands the mathematical model to encompass all sources of measurement error, including systematic bias ($e_s$), random errors ($e_a$), and resolution ($r/2$).

## Sensitivity Coefficients
* When a dependent variable $Y$ relies on multiple independent variables $X_j$, a first-order Taylor expansion is used to model small variations.
* **Sensitivity Coefficient ($c_j$):** The partial derivative of the mathematical function $G$ with respect to $X_j$, calculated at the mean value. It determines how sensitive the output $Y$ is to variations in $X_j$.

$$
c_j = \left( \frac{\partial G}{\partial x_j} \right)_{\bar{x}_j}
$$

## Propagation of Variance
> [!IMPORTANT] Assumption of Independence
> If influence quantities are strongly random and independent, covariances within the variance-covariance matrix are considered null (zero).

The General Law of Variance Propagation allows the calculation of the dependent variable's variance:

$$
s^2(y) = \sum_{j=1}^{q} c_j^2 s^2(x_j)
$$

### Special Case 1: Uncertainty of Differences
* If a quantity is determined as the difference between two others ($Y = X_2 - X_1$), the variance *adds up*, it does not subtract.

$$
s^2(y) = s^2(x_1) + s^2(x_2)
$$

* The closer two quantities are to each other in value, the higher the *relative* standard uncertainty becomes.

### Special Case 2: Multiplicities (Averages)
* **Output Multiplicity ($n_r$):** Used when you have the variance of individual data ($s^2(x)$) but the test procedure requires the final result to be the average of $n_r$ tests.

$$
s^2(m) = \frac{s^2(x)}{n_r}
$$

* **Input Multiplicity ($n_d$):** Used when the received variability information (e.g., from a calibration certificate) refers to an average of $n_d$ tests, but you need the variance of the individual data.

$$
s^2(x) = n_d \cdot s^2(m)
$$

## The Uncertainty Budget Table
* The table framework unifies Type A ($s^2$) and Type B (equivalent variance) contributions into a single **standard uncertainty** ($u$) budget.
* The total combined variance is calculated as:

$$
u^2(y) = \sum_{j=1}^{q} c_j^2 u^2(x_j) = \sum_{j=1}^{q} u_j^2(y)
$$

### 4 Rules for the Budget Table
1. **Unit Homogeneity:** Use the exact same multiple/submultiple for all measurement units to prevent calculation errors, automatically fixing the units of the sensitivity coefficients.
2. **Absolute vs. Relative:** Strictly distinguish between uncertainty contributions given in absolute form (with units) and relative form (percentages).
3. **Classification:** Type A relies on statistical info (standard deviations, experimental data), whereas Type B relies on non-statistical info (resolution, standard tolerances, handbook ranges).
4. **Multiplicities:** Always account for input ($n_d$) and output ($n_r$) averaging conditions when calculating the individual variances $u^2(x_j)$.

## Process Optimization
* The uncertainty budget table visualizes the exact mathematical weight of each contribution ($u_j^2(y)$).
* **To decrease overall uncertainty:** Identify and improve/control the factor with the highest variance contribution (e.g., applying temperature control).
* **To decrease operational costs:** Identify the factors with the lowest variance contributions and relax their tolerances or use cheaper instruments (e.g., using an instrument with coarser resolution if its mathematical impact is negligible).
