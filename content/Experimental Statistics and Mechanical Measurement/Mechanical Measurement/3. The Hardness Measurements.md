## Basic Concepts
* **Definition:** Resistance a material opposes to the separation of its particles (Mohs, 1812) or the normal pressure over a contact area at the elasticity limit (Hertz, 1895).
* **Why Hardness Tests?** Simple, cheap, quick, (semi) non-destructive, and performed directly on the final component with limited sample preparation required.
* **Traceability:** Hardness scales are conventional. Traceability is maintained via direct calibration of the machine (force transducer, depth scale, indenter geometry) or indirect calibration using reference blocks provided by Metrological Institutes.

## Hardness Scales

### 1. Brinell Scale (1900)
* **Indenter:** Spherical steel or tungsten carbide (WC) ball ($D$ = 10 mm).
* **Procedure:** Press the ball with force $F$, then optically measure two orthogonal diameters of the plastic indentation to find the mean diameter $d$.
* **Formula:** Ratio of load $P$ to the area of the spherical cap indentation surface $S$:

$$
HB = 0.102 \cdot \frac{2 \cdot F}{\pi \cdot D(D - \sqrt{D^2 - d^2})}
$$

* **Designation:** XX HBW D/P (e.g., HBW 10/3000).
* **Pros/Cons:** Correlates well to ultimate strength, yield strength, and resilience. Cons: Not automatic, limited by high-hardness materials, and the optical measurement relies on Numerical Aperture (NA) which causes edge shadow uncertainty.

### 2. Rockwell Scale (1920)
* **Indenter:** Diamond cone (120°, $R$ = 200 µm) or hardmetal/steel balls.
* **Procedure:** 
    1. Apply pre-load $F_0$ to establish a zero datum and stabilize contact.
    2. Apply additional load $F_1$ for a dwell time.
    3. Remove $F_1$ (to eliminate elastic deformation) and automatically measure final penetration depth $h$.
* **Formula:** Based purely on depth (e.g., for scales A, C, D):

$$
HR = 100 - \frac{h}{0.002}
$$

* **Pros/Cons:** Fast and automatic. Cons: Purely conventional (loses true metrological characteristics) and the diamond cone is hard to manufacture perfectly.

### 3. Vickers Scale (1935)
* **Indenter:** Diamond squared-pyramid with a 136° apex dihedral angle (chosen to match the tangent of an ideal Brinell spherical cap).
* **Procedure:** Press the indenter, then measure the mean diagonal $d$ of the indentation.
* **Formula** ($S$ = indentation area):

$$
HV = 0.189 \cdot \frac{F}{d^2}
$$

* **Pros/Cons:** Constant aspect ratio regardless of size, and works for high-hardness materials. Cons: Critical optical measurement of diagonals and potential tip offset errors.

### 4. Knoop Scale (1939)
* **Target:** Brittle and fragile materials (glass, ceramics).
* **Indenter:** Diamond rectangular pyramid with a rhombus base (172° 30' and 130° angles).
* **Formula** (where $c \approx 0.0703$):

$$
HK = 1.45 \cdot \frac{F}{d^2}
$$

### 5. Shore Scale
* **Target:** Elastomers and rubber.
* **Traceability:** Not maintained by National Metrological Institutes; relies entirely on direct calibration of the indentation machine.

### 6. Martens Scale / Instrumented Indentation Test (IIT) (2000)
* **Procedure:** Continuous, depth-sensing measurement of applied force $F$ and depth $h$ during loading, holding, and unloading phases to create an indentation curve.
* **Indenters:** Vickers, Berkovich (trigonal diamond), hard metal ball, or diamond ball.
* **Formula:**

$$
HM = \frac{F}{A_s(h)}
$$

* **Pros/Cons:** Provides thorough mechanical characterization (Young's modulus $E$, Hardness $H$, $\sigma$-$\epsilon$ curve). Cons: Nano-IIT requires a strictly controlled metrological room.
