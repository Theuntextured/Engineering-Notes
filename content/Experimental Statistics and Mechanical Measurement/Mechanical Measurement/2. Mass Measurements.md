## Basic Concepts of Mass
* Mass cannot be directly measured. Traceability is instead obtained through the measurement of force.
* **New SI Definition (2019):** The kilogram is defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the Planck constant $h$ to be $6.626 070 15 \times 10^{-34} \text{ kg} \cdot \text{m}^2 \cdot \text{s}^{-1}$. 
    * This replaced the International Kilogram Prototype (the platinum-iridium cylinder stored at BIPM).
    * **Primary Realization:** Achieved using a Kibble balance, which compares a gravitational force with an equivalent electromagnetic force with extreme precision (uncertainties ~10 ppb).

## Force Transducers (Load Cells)
* Modern electronic balances use force transducers (load cells) based on strain gauges.
* These devices transduce mechanical elastic deformation into an electrical signal. 
* High-precision transducers use metallic materials, as semiconductors are overly sensitive to temperature variations.

## Gravity Force vs. Weight Force
* **Gravity Force:** The force generated purely by gravity in a vacuum ($F_g = m \cdot g_l$).
* **Weight Force:** The actual force measured during weighing operations in a fluid (like air). It must account for Archimedes' buoyancy:

$$
F_p = m \cdot g_l \left(1 - \frac{\rho_a}{\rho_m}\right)
$$

* Where $\rho_a$ is air density and $\rho_m$ is the density of the object (measurand).

## The "Conventional Mass" Solution
Managing exact air and object densities in daily practice is impossible. Therefore, the "conventional mass value" ($m_t$) was introduced.
* $m_t$ is the value of an ideal standard mass with a density of $8000 \text{ kg/m}^3$ ($\rho_{mt}$).
* It assumes measurement in standard air with a density of $1.20 \text{ kg/m}^3$ ($\rho_{at}$).
* **Practical Effect:** The conventional mass of the measurand is substantially equal to the conventional mass of the standard because the corrective terms cancel out, making daily mass comparisons feasible.
* *Note:* The actual density of the object ($\rho_m$) only causes a significant error if the object has a very low density (e.g., highly porous or multi-material components). The density of the air ($\rho_a$) fluctuates based on thermodynamic states (e.g., $CO_2$ content) and is only critical when calibrating reference masses.

## The Effect of Gravity Acceleration ($g$)
Balances that measure weight force via transducers are directly affected by variations in local gravity acceleration ($g_l$).

* $g_l$ varies based on latitude (centrifugal rotation) and altitude (distance from the Earth's center).
* Every 1-meter height variation causes $g$ to change by approximately $3 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m/s}^2$. 
    * *Example:* Moving a balance up just ~3 floors ($10 \text{ m}$) can change the measured weight by several milligrams.
* Additionally, local subsoil mineral density creates gravity anomalies, mapped by national gravimetric networks.
* **Industrial Management:** Instead of performing expensive on-site calibrations everywhere, WELMEC proposes subdividing territories into conventional zones (defined by latitude and altitude limits) where variations in $g$ do not exceed the permitted error bounds for the specific balance.
